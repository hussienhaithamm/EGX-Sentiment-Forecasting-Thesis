\chapter{Related Work}
\label{chap:related}

This chapter surveys prior work on sentiment-based stock forecasting. The chapter is organised around four threads: the shift from price-only to text-aware prediction, social-media sentiment, financial-news sentiment, and domain-specific or Arabic-specialised language models. Each thread is summarised with quantitative outcomes from the original work, then critiqued against the bilingual EGX setting that motivates this thesis. The final two sections discuss recurrent failure modes in sentiment-based forecasting and locate the present thesis inside the remaining research gap.

\section{From Price-Only to Text-Aware Forecasting}

Stock-prediction research did not begin with sentiment. Early systems relied on prices, returns, volume, and technical indicators because those variables are directly observable and easy to align temporally. The main weakness of that tradition is explanatory rather than computational. Numerical series encode market state, but they do not encode the semantic content of the events that moved the market. Once researchers began treating news and social media as external information channels, forecasting turned into a multimodal problem rather than a purely numerical one \cite{fischer2018lstm, nelson2017lstm, sezer2020dlreview}.

The shift from price-only to text-aware prediction followed a recognisable sequence. The AZFinText system used breaking financial news to support short-term directional prediction and showed that textual features add value when the entity linkage is explicit and the horizon is short \cite{schumaker2009azfin}. A later context-capturing study improved on bag-of-words pipelines for financial news classification \cite{hagenau2013automated}. Subsequent work replaced manual text features with event embeddings, hierarchical attention, and latent-variable sequence models \cite{ding2015event, hu2018chaotic, xu2018stock}. Structured event representations modelled how short-term and long-term event effects influenced price movement \cite{ding2015event}. A hierarchical attention model captured news sequences \cite{hu2018chaotic}. A deep generative framework fused tweets and price history and reported new state-of-the-art results on a newly collected dataset \cite{xu2018stock}. The common lesson is that text representation, entity resolution, and temporal alignment are as important as the downstream predictor.

\section{Social Media Sentiment}

Social media attracted sustained attention because it is timely, large in volume, and rich in investor reaction. A landmark mood-based study analysed approximately 9.8 million tweets from February to December 2008 and linked extracted mood dimensions to subsequent movements in the Dow Jones Industrial Average (DJIA) \cite{bollen2011twitter}. The best reported directional result reached 87.6\% accuracy after including mood information. The setting is index-level rather than firm-level, so aggregate mood can correlate with broad market movement without solving the harder task of mapping noisy user text to specific equities.

A later message-board study moved closer to firm-level prediction by combining company-related posts with topic-aware sentiment features \cite{nguyen2015social}. The experiment covered 18 stocks over one year, with 171 transaction dates for training and 78 for testing. The topic-sentiment method improved mean accuracy by 2.07 percentage points across the 18 stocks and by 9.83 percentage points on the subset of difficult stocks relative to a price-only baseline. This result quantifies the value of better ticker-specific filtering. The setup remains short-horizon, single-language, and dependent on shallow topic extraction rather than contextual language models.

A multi-signal comparison study evaluated survey sentiment, news headlines, Twitter data, and search-engine signals in the same predictive setting \cite{mao2011predicting}. The main reported finding is that different sources behave differently in time, with search and social signals more informative for short-run market behaviour than traditional survey measures. Sentiment source is therefore not an interchangeable design choice. Speed, topic granularity, and user noise all affect what a sentiment variable actually measures.

The aggregate social-media literature shows that sentiment carries predictive information only after heavy filtering. Short text length, sarcasm, reposted content, unofficial company references, and bot-driven amplification all weaken the link between message sentiment and tradable firm-specific information \cite{bollen2011twitter, nguyen2015social, albahli2022twitter}. These constraints become sharper for emerging markets with bilingual discussion, because entity linking and language variation must be solved before modelling begins.

Arabic social-media forecasting work is especially relevant to the future direction of this thesis. A correlation and Granger-causality study examined Arabic tweets and Arab stock-market indices, including EGX30 \cite{alkhatib2016arabictweets}. The reported associations support the idea that Arabic investor discussion may carry market-relevant information for Egyptian equities. The same study reinforces the need for careful source access and entity filtering. Social-media relevance depends on whether the collected posts can be legally obtained, temporally aligned, and linked to the correct market or company.

\section{Financial News Sentiment}

Financial news is a cleaner signal source than social media because the language is more structured and the company linkage is more explicit. A large-scale S\&P 500 panel study assembled five years of daily prices for S\&P 500 firms together with more than 265{,}000 related financial news articles \cite{mohan2019news}. Deep models built from price data, sentiment information, and hybrid text-price inputs were then compared. The scale of the dataset is a strength because many earlier sentiment studies were limited by sparse article counts. External validity is limited, because a large U.S.\ large-cap news panel is an easier information environment than a thinner bilingual EGX news stream.

A controlled architecture study compared LSTM and GRU models under identical conditions with and without financial news sentiment \cite{dahal2023comparative}. Performance was assessed with Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE), and the correlation coefficient $R$. The sentiment-augmented variants outperformed stock-only variants, which isolates the effect of adding text. The study focuses on regression quality for developed-market data, not on leakage-sensitive directional classification for sparse firm-level coverage.

A multi-representation fusion study combined sentiment analysis with candlestick-chart representations in a two-branch Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) \cite{huang2021candlestick}. Experiments covered five high-demand U.S.\ stocks, namely Apple, Tesla, IBM, Amazon, and Google, with forecast windows of 4, 6, 8, and 10 days. The best reported accuracy reached 75.38\% for Apple. Representation fusion outperformed either visual price input or sentiment input alone in that setting. The result is stock-specific, U.S.-centric, and uses social-media sentiment rather than a firm-linked financial-news archive.

A separate regression-fit study used market-news sentiment as an exogenous factor in opening-price models \cite{cristescu2022marketnews}. One reported nonlinear autoregressive exogenous (NARX) specification achieved an Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) significance level of $p = 0.020$. Sentiment improved model fit in that setting. Regression fit is not the same as robust directional forecasting, because a model can improve opening-price estimation while still failing to discriminate reliably between upward and downward moves.

A deeper fusion system, SA-DLSTM, combined an emotion-enhanced CNN, a denoising autoencoder, and an LSTM for stock-movement prediction on the Hang Seng Index \cite{zhao2023integrated}. The system captures three design ideas that recur in later literature: sentiment-strength weighting, feature denoising, and sequence modelling over multivariate financial inputs. The target remains an index-level market series, which avoids the harder problem of article-to-firm relevance matching that is central to the present thesis.

The most relevant benchmark-oriented reference for this thesis is a broad horizon-comparative study \cite{liapis2023deep}. The study evaluated 30 forecasting methods over 67 feature setups and three forecast horizons. The reported conclusion is that no single sentiment configuration dominates all horizons. Sentiment effectiveness is conditional on representation and horizon choice. The same finding reinforces the need for conservative reporting, because a sentiment setup that performs well for one horizon may be statistically unremarkable or weak at another.

\section{Why Sentiment Does Not Always Improve Forecasting}

The surveyed literature does not support a simple claim that sentiment always improves predictive accuracy. Four failure modes recur.

The first failure mode is semantic coarseness. A positive article can describe a low-impact corporate event, while a seemingly neutral article can contain material financing or governance information. A market-attitude study argued that sentiment polarity alone does not consistently explain future price changes and that any useful relation is strongly case-specific \cite{mudinas2019market}. Treating polarity as a sufficient event representation is therefore unsafe.

The second failure mode is temporal misalignment. If article sentiment is joined to prices on the wrong day, or if a target window overlaps the feature window, reported performance can be inflated. The horizon-comparative benchmark shows that different sentiment feature setups behave differently across horizons \cite{liapis2023deep}. Loose alignment can therefore distort comparisons by turning methodological noise into apparent predictive signal.

The third failure mode is data scarcity and imbalance. Sparse firm-level article panels create unstable feature histories, and directional labels often lean toward the majority class. A model can then achieve acceptable raw accuracy by overpredicting the dominant direction \cite{shi2021deeplearning, liapis2023deep}. This issue is especially relevant for emerging markets where article volume is much lower than in U.S.\ large-cap datasets. The combination of sparse text and imbalanced labels makes balanced accuracy and confusion-matrix analysis more informative than headline accuracy alone.

The fourth failure mode is source mismatch. Social media is fast and abundant, but it is noisy and weakly linked to formal firm events. Financial news is cleaner and more structured, but it is slower and less dense. The contrast between the DJIA mood, message-board topic, and multi-signal studies on one side and the S\&P 500 panel, LSTM-vs-GRU, and NARX studies on the other shows that sentiment source changes both the signal quality and the methodological difficulty \cite{bollen2011twitter, nguyen2015social, mao2011predicting, mohan2019news, dahal2023comparative, cristescu2022marketnews}. No source is universally superior. The choice must match the research objective, the market, and the entity-linking assumptions.

\section{Domain-Specific and Arabic Language Models}

The quality of the sentiment signal depends heavily on the language model used to produce it. Generic sentiment tools often misread financial language because words such as ``liability'', ``dilution'', or ``guidance'' carry domain-specific meanings. FinBERT was designed precisely for this issue. The original paper reports a 14 percentage point improvement over the previous state of the art on Financial PhraseBank, which makes it a much stronger candidate for financial English than a generic sentiment classifier \cite{araci2019finbert}. Weak sentence-level sentiment estimation propagates directly into weaker daily forecasting features.

The Arabic side requires an additional specialisation step. AraBERT reported state-of-the-art performance on most tested Arabic NLP tasks, with improvements of up to 2.53 F1 points on the downstream benchmarks \cite{antoun2020arabert}. ARBERT and MARBERT extended this line further and introduced the ARLUE benchmark, where the best model achieved an aggregate score of 77.40 and outperformed XLM-R Large despite the latter being substantially larger \cite{abdulmageed2021arbert}. These results justify routing Arabic text through Arabic-specialised encoders rather than treating Arabic as a secondary case inside a generic multilingual pipeline.

Variant sensitivity is equally important. A CAMeLBERT comparison across five task types and 12 datasets concluded that variant proximity between pre-training data and downstream data matters more than raw pre-training size \cite{inoue2021camelbert}. This finding is directly relevant for bilingual EGX forecasting. The problem is not only to classify sentiment, but to classify it in a market where Arabic and English articles occupy different stylistic and domain positions. A single shared model is simpler to deploy but comes at the cost of linguistic fit.

The Arabic-model literature leaves an open gap. These models are evaluated on Arabic NLP benchmarks, not on bilingual financial forecasting benchmarks for the Egyptian market. Strong benchmark performance justifies their use as sentiment encoders. It does not by itself prove that they improve stock prediction. That remaining empirical question is one of the contributions of the present thesis \cite{antoun2020arabert, abdulmageed2021arbert, inoue2021camelbert}.

Recent Arabic financial NLP resources make this gap more visible. The SAHM benchmark introduces Arabic financial and Shari'ah-compliant reasoning tasks \cite{elbadry2026sahm}. Recent Saudi-market work studies Arabic financial sentiment with large language models \cite{albaqawi2026arabicfinancialsentiment}. Both resources support the need for Arabic finance-specific evaluation rather than reliance on general Arabic sentiment benchmarks. Both also clarify a limitation of the present thesis: without independent human-labeled Arabic financial sentiment examples, AraBERT can be adapted to financial text through Masked Language Modeling (MLM), but it cannot be validly evaluated as a supervised Arabic FinBERT equivalent.

\section{Evaluation Metrics and Research Gap}

Comparison across prior forecasting studies is complicated by inconsistent evaluation practice. Regression-oriented papers often report RMSE, MAE, MAPE, or correlation. Directional papers more often report accuracy, precision, recall, and F1. A few papers add simulated returns, which is useful but depends strongly on trading assumptions. These metrics are not interchangeable. A classifier can show a favourable raw accuracy while failing to recover the minority class. A regression model can reduce RMSE while still predicting the sign of the move poorly. Metrics must therefore be interpreted in relation to the target definition and the class balance \cite{dahal2023comparative, liapis2023deep, shi2021deeplearning, zhao2023integrated}.

The gap addressed by this thesis is both geographical and methodological. Most sentiment-based forecasting studies focus on English text, developed markets, single-language pipelines, or index-level prediction. Very few studies examine bilingual Arabic-English financial-news routing for Egyptian equities. Fewer still evaluate that problem under conservative walk-forward validation with explicit leakage and overfitting checks. The present work narrows the scope to three EGX tickers and a single bilingual financial-news source so that article relevance, language routing, and temporal alignment can be studied under cleaner conditions. The contribution is not the assumption that sentiment is universally powerful. The contribution is a test, under a more locally relevant and more tightly controlled design, of how much forecasting value bilingual financial-news sentiment actually carries.

\section{Chapter Summary}

This chapter reviewed the state of the art in sentiment-based stock forecasting and identified the lessons that shape the present thesis. Text helps, but reported gains depend on source quality, feature design, horizon choice, and validation rigour. Financial-news studies are cleaner than social-media studies, yet they remain concentrated in English and in developed markets. Domain-specific financial English models and Arabic-specialised transformers offer strong upstream sentiment encoders, but they were rarely evaluated together in a bilingual EGX forecasting setting. These gaps motivate the methodological choices developed in the next chapter.
