\chapter{Background}
\label{chap:background}

This chapter introduces the ideas behind the thesis design. It starts with financial forecasting and limited predictability, then narrows to directional labels, class imbalance, financial-news sentiment, domain-specific NLP, Arabic and bilingual text processing, and leakage-safe evaluation. The goal is to show why the method in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} avoids price-derived features and uses strict chronological testing.

\section{Financial Forecasting and Limited Predictability}

Financial forecasting sits between economics, statistics, and machine learning. Prices react to public information, liquidity, expectations, and strategic trading. The efficient-market view argues that public information is rapidly incorporated into prices, which makes persistent predictability difficult \cite{fama1970}. Later work questions the strongest random-walk interpretation, but still supports the view that predictable structure is weak and unstable \cite{lo1988randomwalk}.

This matters because a short-horizon forecasting result should not be accepted only because it has attractive raw accuracy. A useful study must define the target carefully, avoid future information, and compare against simple baselines. In this thesis, the target is not a future price level. It is the direction of the EGX30 index over short trading-day horizons.

The distinction is important. Predicting a price level asks the model to estimate magnitude and direction at the same time. Direction classification asks a narrower question: whether the future index return is positive or negative. That narrower target fits the goal of this thesis because the study tests whether news sentiment has directional information, not whether it can estimate the size of the next market move.

\section{Directional Labels and Input Modalities}

Forecasting studies use several targets, including price level, return magnitude, return sign, and binary direction. Direction classification is common because it maps naturally to whether the market moved up or down over a horizon. It also loses information about magnitude, so direction results must be interpreted as classification evidence rather than as return forecasts \cite{sezer2020dlreview}.

Input modalities also differ. Price-based models use historical closes, returns, volumes, and technical indicators. Text-aware models use news, disclosures, social media, or analyst commentary. This thesis excludes price-derived predictors from the model matrix so that the news signal can be tested before any future multimodal extension.

\section{Class Imbalance and Evaluation Sensitivity}

Directional labels are often imbalanced. In an upward-trending period, always predicting an Up move can produce high raw accuracy while failing to discriminate between market states. Balanced accuracy is central here because it averages performance over both classes and is less forgiving when a classifier collapses toward the majority class.

F1, precision, recall, Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), confusion-matrix counts, actual Up share, and predicted Up share provide additional context. These metrics do not replace balanced accuracy, but they help explain whether a model is learning a sentiment relation or simply following the dominant class. Stronger naive baselines are therefore part of the evaluation design rather than an optional appendix.

This issue is not only statistical. It changes the story a result can support. A model that mostly predicts Up may look useful in a rising market, but it has not necessarily learned when sentiment separates positive and negative future sessions. The thesis therefore treats class balance as part of the research question, not as a small reporting detail.

\section{Financial News and Sentiment}

Financial news can describe information that historical prices do not encode directly. Prior work shows that media tone, message-board language, and financial dictionaries can relate to market outcomes, but the usefulness of the signal depends on source quality, entity relevance, and aggregation \cite{tetlock2007media, das2007yahoo, loughran2011liability, kearney2014survey}.

Financial news is usually cleaner than social media because articles are longer, more structured, and more often tied to identifiable companies. It is still imperfect. A company-page article may be weakly related to the firm, repeated across pages, or published outside trading hours. These issues motivate relevance weighting, deduplication, and lagged alignment in the methodology.

\section{Domain-Specific Financial NLP}

General sentiment models can misread financial language. Words such as ``liability'', ``capital'', or ``debt'' may have domain-specific meanings that differ from everyday sentiment. FinBERT addresses this issue for English financial text and is therefore a suitable English sentiment encoder for this thesis \cite{araci2019finbert}.

The choice of sentiment model matters because sentence-level errors accumulate into daily market-mood features. A weak sentiment classifier can create noisy aggregate signals even if the forecasting model is correctly validated. For this reason, the thesis treats sentiment scoring as part of the research method rather than as a black-box preprocessing step.

Domain fit matters more in finance than in many general text tasks. A word that sounds negative in everyday language may be neutral in a balance-sheet context, and a short earnings headline may carry information that a generic sentiment model treats as ordinary business language. This is why the English side uses a financial model instead of a general sentiment classifier.

\section{Arabic and Bilingual Financial NLP}

Arabic financial NLP adds linguistic complexity. Arabic is morphologically rich and appears across formal, dialectal, and domain-specific variants. Arabic-specific transformer models such as AraBERT, ARBERT, MARBERT, and CAMeLBERT show that language and variant proximity can matter for downstream NLP performance \cite{antoun2020arabert, abdulmageed2021arbert, inoue2021camelbert}.

The EGX setting is bilingual. English articles and Arabic articles are not simply translations of the same information stream. They may differ in timing, wording, and coverage. This justifies a language-routing design: English articles are scored with an English financial model, while Arabic articles are scored with Arabic encoders and compared in the final evaluation.

This also means that bilingual processing is not just a translation problem. Translating all Arabic articles into English would add another source of error and could remove local financial wording. Routing each language through a more suitable model keeps the original text closer to its market context.

\section{Chronological Evaluation and Leakage}

Time-series forecasting is vulnerable to leakage. Future information can enter through same-day article alignment, overlapping target windows, hyperparameter tuning on test data, or refitting with labels that would not yet be known at prediction time. Text data makes this harder because articles have timestamps while targets are defined on exchange trading calendars \cite{liapis2023deep, dahal2023comparative}.

Chronological validation is therefore part of the problem definition. The thesis uses train, validation, and test regions in time order, adds embargo buffers, tunes thresholds on validation predictions only, and generates validation and test predictions through walk-forward histories. These choices make the reported results harder to inflate and easier to audit.

\section{Chapter Summary}

This chapter established the background logic for the thesis. Short-horizon market direction classification is difficult, sentiment is potentially useful but noisy, and bilingual EGX news requires language-aware processing. Evaluation must prioritize balanced accuracy and leakage control because raw accuracy can be misleading. The next chapter reviews related work and identifies the specific gap addressed by this thesis.
